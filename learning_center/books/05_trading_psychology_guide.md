# Trading Psychology — Practical Guide

## 1. Process vs. Outcome
- A single trade's result (win or loss) is a poor measure of decision quality, because a well-executed, rule-following trade can still lose (variance), and a rule-breaking trade can still win (luck).
- Evaluating trades on **process** (did I follow my plan, my entry criteria, my risk rules?) rather than purely on **outcome** (did it make money?) is the foundation of building consistency, because outcome-only evaluation reinforces whatever behavior happened to work last time, good habit or bad.

## 2. Common Destructive Patterns
- **FOMO (Fear of Missing Out):** entering a trade *after* a big move has already happened, driven by the discomfort of watching it move without you — this typically means entering at the worst possible risk/reward point, often right before a pullback.
- **Revenge trading:** immediately re-entering the market after a loss, at larger size or with looser criteria, in an attempt to "win it back." This compounds losses because it is a reaction to emotion rather than a valid setup.
- **Overtrading:** taking many more trades than the strategy's actual edge calls for, often from boredom or the belief that "more attempts = more profit," which usually just means more transaction costs and more exposure to low-quality setups.
- **Moving stops further away:** widening a stop-loss after entry because the trade is "about to work," which quietly turns a defined-risk trade into an undefined-risk one at the worst possible moment.
- **Confirmation bias:** after forming an opinion on direction, unconsciously seeking out only the evidence that supports it and dismissing evidence against it.

## 3. Discipline Mechanisms
- **Written trading plan:** entry criteria, stop rules, position sizing rules, and daily/weekly loss limits written down *before* trading, reducing in-the-moment improvisation.
- **Trade journal:** logging the setup, reasoning, screenshots, and outcome for every trade creates a factual record to review, separate from memory (which tends to be distorted, especially around losses).
- **Pre-defined "no-trade" conditions:** explicitly listing conditions under which no trade should be taken (e.g., major news release in the next N minutes, daily loss limit already hit, no clear setup present) removes ambiguity in the heat of the moment.
- **Checklists:** requiring a setup to tick every box on a written checklist before entry reduces impulsive, criteria-skipping entries.

## 4. Handling Losing Streaks
- Losing streaks are a statistical certainty for any strategy with a win rate under 100% — the relevant question is whether losses stayed within the pre-defined risk limits, not whether losses happened at all.
- Reducing size (rather than stopping entirely or increasing size) during a losing streak is a common middle-ground approach: it preserves capital while still allowing the strategy to prove or disprove itself over a reasonable sample size.
- Reviewing whether losses came from valid setups that simply didn't work out (normal variance) versus rule-breaking or setup misidentification (a process problem) should shape the response very differently.

## 5. Emotional Regulation Basics
- Recognizing physical/emotional signals of an activated state (racing thoughts, urge to "get it back immediately," tunnel vision on one instrument) as a cue to pause rather than act is a practical, learnable skill.
- Stepping away from the screen after a significant loss or a string of losses — rather than immediately re-entering — is a simple, low-cost habit that prevents many of the worst decisions in trading.
- Separating trading capital from money needed for near-term living expenses reduces the psychological pressure that turns ordinary variance into panic-driven decisions.

## 6. Realistic Expectations
- Consistent profitability (if achieved at all) typically develops over a long timeframe with significant variance along the way — expecting steady, linear gains from week one sets up disappointment that can drive rule-breaking.
- No amount of psychological discipline compensates for a strategy with no real statistical edge; discipline determines whether a *good* strategy's edge is actually realized, not whether a bad strategy becomes profitable.

## Important Caveat
This guide describes common behavioral patterns discussed in trading psychology literature; it is not a substitute for professional mental-health support if trading-related stress, compulsive behavior, or financial loss is causing significant distress. This material is educational only and is not financial or psychological advice.
